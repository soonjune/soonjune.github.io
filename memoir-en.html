Thirty Years of Life: A Memoir

Written in 2023 · translated from Korean · 한국어 원문

Introduction

This is an essay about the choices we meet as we go through life. As the saying goes, life is the Choice between Birth and Death, and this essay is about what a person should weigh in order to choose. Korea adopted the international age system this year, 2023, so my age dropped from 31 to 29. I have lived close to thirty years: I finished high school, went through undergraduate and graduate school, and found a job. I want to write down what I have realized along the way. Confucius called thirty 而立, the age at which one stands firm in one's purpose, so here I set down the homespun philosophy I have built up so far.

Most of it is written by connecting things I learned during my master's degree. I studied reinforcement learning, which deals with how an AI receives feedback on its choices and gradually makes better ones. That left me wanting to try explaining what I studied by relating it to life. The writing wanders somewhat, but I hope the overall message comes through more than the specifics. Because this is a memoir, a lot of it is personal and miscellaneous. Even if it is not the easiest read, you can look at the contents and read only the parts that interest you.

Contents

  2. Principle: minimizing regret
  3. Present vs. future
  4. Principles of choice

1. My life strategy

In an undergraduate business class, I once wrote my own life strategy. A strategy has to be revised constantly to fit the situation, but I think the exercise helped me set my own principles for life. A life principle is the standard you base your choices on at the many forks in the road. Of course, some principles can be compromised and some cannot. The idea of life principles also appears in Principles, the book by the successful investor Ray Dalio. Keeping to principles does not guarantee success, but a choice made "for a reason" is different from one made "for no reason". If I work out concretely what I want and choose accordingly, would that not bring me at least a little closer to the future I hope for? Below is the principle I wrote in that business class four years ago. I defined a "life function" and expressed the principle as optimizing it.

maximize f(J, −F, P) subject to Decent Living Condition

Here the function stood for the meaningfulness of a life. A "meaningful" life is an abstract thing to define, but I put in three elements to describe what meaning in life is made of.

J is the job: how much pride I can take in my work. Work is a large part of life, so how rewarding I find it seems hard to argue with. That reward can be expressed as how much positive effect my work has on other people. There is also the satisfaction you feel on your own, but personally I dreamed of a life that has a good influence on others.

F is family, and the minus sign is there because it stands for the absence of family problems, not for family happiness. Like the famous line from Anna Karenina, "Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way," I made the condition not being an unhappy family, as opposed to being a happy one. I now think it could be a constraint of the optimization problem and not an input to the function.

P is pleasure: short-term enjoyment. It shows a certain greed (?) for not giving up present enjoyment for the sake of the future. Next to it I wrote "carpe diem", meaning I would also treasure short-term joys such as good food, dating and games.

The last condition, a Decent Living Condition, means an adequate income. I quoted Mencius, 無恒産 無恒心 (without a steady livelihood there is no steady heart), and wrote that you need to be reasonably well fed before you can find happiness in life. A similar Korean proverb says that generosity comes from a full rice jar. I seem to have thought that I had to be able to make a living first before I could look after others and be happy. I consider it the most basic condition and an important one.

I wrote this four years ago, but I think it is not very different from what I want now. And I think other people probably want something similar. I have drawn the big picture of what I want, but whether that actually helps is another question. A strategy is designed from the top down, so it can hardly cover every situation. For the choices we face in everyday life, setting principles may help more than a strategy. Still, before setting principles, why not first draw up a "life strategy" that captures what you want and what kind of life you want to live?

2. Principle: minimizing regret

I think my view of life as optimization was influenced by the regret minimization of Amazon founder Jeff Bezos. Besides the life strategy above, I believe it is important to make the choices that minimize regret, so I made it my first principle. For the regret minimization framework itself, a Google search will do. Here I want to talk about regret as I understand it.

During my master's I happened to come across the concept of regret. I studied a family of algorithms called multi-armed bandits, and I want to introduce the concept of "regret" that appears there. (I will not explain what a multi-armed bandit is here. If you are curious, I recommend this post, in Korean.)

A bandit algorithm is evaluated by its regret. The algorithm that makes regret smallest is the better one, and regret is defined in terms of reward as follows.

regret = the reward from the best choice available to me − the reward from the choice I made

Each choice produces a regret of zero or more, and if I only ever make the best choice, regret is zero, which is the best outcome. Realistically, it is impossible to make only the best choices. How could we know whether the dish we picked for lunch was the best choice, or what reward the other dishes would have given? The algorithm also has the limitation that it only works where rewards are clearly defined and can be measured exactly. Still, if we were to write regret as a formula, I doubt anyone would object that it would look something like the above.

We feel "regret" when we believed our choice was the best and then learn that a better option existed. Since regret is something we recognize in the future, you might think it is no help in choosing now. That is why I believe we need the imagination to picture where a choice will take us. I also believe it is important to settle clearly which factors matter to me, that is, which factors affect my reward.

Choices usually involve trade-offs: choosing one thing means giving up another. From the next principle on, let us look at the factors at play in those situations. "Minimize regret" is a kind of declarative principle, the most important one, and it governs the principles that follow. In the actual process of choosing, the principles derived from trade-offs may matter more. Realistically, when I do not know what reward a choice will bring, it is more important to decide what determines my reward, and which principles follow from that, than to try to settle the regret right away. So the principles that follow are not stated in a single phrase like "minimize regret". They are written in "vs." form, so that each person can decide what matters to them within the trade-off.

3. Present vs. future

People differ in how much weight they give the present and how much the future. This usually shows up through the concept of a discount rate. It is the same idea as the interest rate in economics: with a high discount rate, you pursue the pleasure available right now over a larger pleasure in the future.

When we compute expected future returns, we also multiply the possible return by a probability. Applied to a person's life, that probability can only be subjective. How accurate can a probability assigned to an uncertain future be! Valuing a company also involves discounting expected future earnings into present value, and subjectivity and intuition come into it. I suppose that is why company valuation is sometimes called an "art".

The phrase sohwakhaeng (small but certain happiness), popular for some years now, also shows that people are becoming more averse to uncertainty.

4. Principles of choice

I have gone on at length, but I think that, in the end, the rules above are also there for my own happiness. Yet happiness is far too abstract, and people are not rational, so making a perfect choice is close to impossible. What I have felt in living thirty-odd years is that I must guard against the "trap of perfectionism". There is no such thing as a perfect life. Even so, the attitude of not losing heart and carving out the life you want deserves praise.

I have lived alone, and now that I am thinking about starting a family, I realize the principles I have held so far were written only from my own point of view. When I have someone I love, and children, how will my values change? What I hold dear will change, and there will come moments when I have to give things up. My parents must have made countless sacrifices for me. As I interact with more people and the order of the equation rises, it will get harder to solve. Even if I cannot find the optimal solution, I should keep trying to find an approximate one.

It has turned into an odd ending: I began by discussing principles and I am closing with a resolution. I sometimes wonder how reckless it is to lay down principles for something that has no right answer. Still, even if the principles are not perfect, perhaps my own philosophy can at least serve as an approximate solution. The AI that is on the rise now does not guarantee right answers either. The hallucinations of large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT are the best-known example. But just as AI models have brought humanity a great deal of use through more data and steady improvement, the principles of choice, too, should stay flexible enough to change with circumstances.
